Chords

Piano and keyboard chords, and their inversions

On a keyboard, chords are easier to understand than on any other instrument, because the notes are laid out in a line and every chord of one type has the same spacing. If you can count to seven, you can find any major or minor chord. This page shows how, and then how to rearrange chords so that your hands hardly have to move.

Finding your way round the keys

The black keys come in groups of two and three. C is the white key just to the left of any group of two black keys. The white keys then run C, D, E, F, G, A, B and repeat. Each black key has two names: the one between C and D is C sharp or D flat. Moving from any key to the very next key, black or white, is one semitone.

Major and minor chords by counting

  • Major chord: play the root, count up 4 semitones for the second note, then 3 more for the third. C major: C, E, G.
  • Minor chord: root, up 3, then up 4. C minor: C, E flat, G.

The outer notes are the same in both; only the middle note moves. Try it on D: D, F sharp, A is major; D, F, A is minor. The recipes for every other chord type are on how chords are built, and each chord page on this site shows the keys to press.

Fingering

Fingers are numbered from the thumb: thumb 1, index 2, middle 3, ring 4, little finger 5.

  • Right hand, three-note chord in root position: fingers 1, 3, 5.
  • Left hand: fingers 5, 3, 1 (little finger on the root).
  • Keep the wrist level and the fingers curved. Press the three keys together, with the weight of the arm, not by poking with the fingers.

Inversions: the same notes in a different order

A chord keeps its name whichever of its notes is at the bottom. Rearranging the notes gives inversions:

To make the next inversion, take the bottom note and move it to the top. A four-note chord has a third inversion as well.

Why inversions matter

Play C major, F major and G major all in root position and your hand jumps about the keyboard. Now try this:

  • C major in root position: C, E, G
  • F major in second inversion: C, F, A (the C stays; the other two notes each move up one step)
  • G major in first inversion: B, D, G (compared with the C chord you started from, the G stays and the two lower notes each move down one step)

The hand stays in one place. The rule is: when changing chords, keep any note the two chords share, and move the other notes to the nearest note of the new chord. This is called voice leading, and it is why a pianist's chords sound smooth and connected.

What the left hand does

  • Single bass note: the root of the chord, low down. Simple and always right.
  • Octave: the root with little finger and thumb, for a fuller sound.
  • Root and fifth: an open, strong sound that fits both major and minor.
  • Broken chord: the notes of the chord played one after another: bottom, top, middle, top.

With the root in the left hand, the right hand is free to play any inversion.

Slash chords

A symbol such as C/E means "C major with E as the lowest note": play E in the left hand and a C chord in the right. Slash chords are simply inversions written out, and they are often used to make the bass line walk by step.

Harmonium and Indian keyboards

On a harmonium the same keys make the same chords, though the instrument is traditionally used for melody with a drone. Players who name notes in sargam can read a major chord as Sa, Ga, Pa and a minor chord as Sa, komal Ga, Pa, counted from whichever key is taken as Sa.

A practice routine

  1. Choose one major chord. Play root position, first inversion, second inversion and root position an octave higher, up and back, with each hand.
  2. Do the same for its minor chord.
  3. Play the three main chords of a key (I, IV, V from chords in every key) with the smallest possible movement.
  4. Add a left-hand root to each.
  5. Take a new key each week.
