Chords

Your first guitar chords: eight open chords

Open chords are chords played near the nut that use some open strings. They are the easiest chords on the guitar, they sound full, and with eight of them you can accompany a very large number of songs. Learn them in the order below: each new chord shares fingers or shapes with one you already know.

Before you start

  • Tune first. A chord played perfectly on an out-of-tune guitar sounds wrong, and you will blame your fingers. Use our neighbour site Tuner.
  • Cut the nails of your fretting hand short.
  • Expect sore fingertips for the first two weeks. Short, frequent practice is better than one long session; the soreness passes as the skin toughens.
  • Read how to read chord diagrams.

The eight chords

What makes each one tricky

  • E minor: two fingers, all six strings. The friendliest chord on the guitar. Start here.
  • A minor: keep the fingers arched so that the open 1st string rings. Do not strum the 6th string.
  • E major: the A minor shape moved across by one string. Notice how alike they feel.
  • D major: a small triangle on the three thin strings. Strum only four strings; the low strings are the usual mistake.
  • A major: three fingers in one fret. Squeeze them in a row or slightly staggered; some players use one flattened finger.
  • C major: a stretch across three frets. Curl the first finger so that the open 3rd and 1st strings sound.
  • G major: the widest stretch. Several fingerings exist; choose the one that lets you change to C most easily.
  • D minor: like D major with one note lowered. Good practice for the little triangle shapes.

Changing between chords

Playing one chord is easy. Music happens in the change. Practise pairs:

  1. E minor to A minor
  2. A minor to C: the first and second fingers stay where they are, and only the third finger moves
  3. C to G
  4. G to D
  5. D to A
  6. A to E

For each pair, set a timer for one minute and count how many clean changes you make. Write the number down. Next day, try to beat it. When you reach thirty changes a minute, the pair is ready for songs. Look for fingers that can stay down or slide along a string between two chords: they are your anchors.

A ten-minute daily plan

  1. 2 minutes: tune, then place each chord you know and pick through its strings one by one to check that every note sounds.
  2. 4 minutes: one-minute changes on four pairs.
  3. 3 minutes: strum a sequence of four chords, four slow down-strokes on each, without stopping, even if a chord is not ready. Keeping the hand moving matters more than a perfect chord.
  4. 1 minute: try the next new chord.

What these chords can do together

Chords that belong to one key sound right together. From these eight you can already play in several keys:

  • Key of G: G, C, D, with E minor and A minor
  • Key of C: C, G, A minor, D minor, E minor (the missing F is the first barre chord most people learn)
  • Key of D: D, G, A, with E minor
  • Key of A: A, D, E
  • Key of E minor or A minor for darker songs

What comes next

Add the seventh chords E7, A7, D7 and B7, which are small changes to shapes you know. Then F major and B minor will introduce you to barre chords, the shapes that unlock every key. If changes feel slow, read changing chords smoothly.
