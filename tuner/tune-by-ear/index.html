How to tune by ear

A tuner is quick, but batteries fail and phones are forgotten, and no tuner can tell you whether two instruments sound good together. Tuning by ear is the older skill and the deeper one. It rests on a single ability, hearing whether two notes are the same, and anyone can learn it with a little practice. This page teaches three methods, from easiest to most exact.

Step 1: get one note right

Every method starts from one trusted note. Use the reference tones on this site (the buttons under each instrument's tuner, or the tone generator), a keyboard, a harmonium, a tuning fork, or another instrument that is already in tune.

Method 1: matching a reference tone

  1. Play the reference tone for the string you are tuning.
  2. Pluck the string and listen to both. Ask one question: is my string lower or higher?
  3. If you cannot tell, loosen the string until it is clearly too low. Now you know which way to go.
  4. Tighten slowly while plucking, until the two notes merge into one.

Humming helps. Hum the reference note, then pluck your string and hum that; the voice feels the difference that the ear misses.

Method 2: the fifth-fret method (guitar)

Once one string is right, a guitar can be tuned to itself, because the same note can be played in two places.

  1. Tune the 6th string (low E) to a reference.
  2. Press the 6th string at the 5th fret. That note is A. Tune the open 5th string to match it.
  3. 5th string, 5th fret, is D: tune the open 4th string to it.
  4. 4th string, 5th fret, is G: tune the open 3rd string to it.
  5. 3rd string, 4th fret (not the 5th), is B: tune the open 2nd string to it. This is the one exception.
  6. 2nd string, 5th fret, is E: tune the open 1st string to it.

Finish by playing the two E strings together: they should sound like one note, two octaves apart. Small errors add up along the chain, so check a chord or two at the end.

The same idea works on other instruments. On a ukulele: the C string at the 4th fret gives E, the E string at the 5th fret gives A, and the E string at the 3rd fret gives the high G. On a bass: each string at the 5th fret gives the next open string.

Method 3: listening for beats

When two notes are almost but not quite the same, they do not simply sound "a bit off". They produce a regular pulsing in loudness, a slow "wah... wah... wah". These are beats, and they are the most precise tool an ear has.

  • The number of beats per second equals the difference between the two frequencies. Notes of 440 and 442 Hz beat twice a second.
  • As you bring the notes together, the beats slow down. When they stop, the notes agree.
  • If the beats speed up as you turn, you are going the wrong way.

Beats are easiest to hear with long, steady notes: two guitar strings ringing, two reeds of a harmonium, the pairs of a mandolin, two bowed violin strings. Piano tuners do their whole job by counting beats.

Harmonics: clearer beats on a guitar

Touch a string lightly, without pressing it down, directly above the 12th, 7th or 5th fret, pluck, and lift the finger: a clear, bell-like note sounds. These are harmonics. They ring for a long time and beat very plainly.

  • The harmonic at the 5th fret of the 6th string and the harmonic at the 7th fret of the 5th string are nearly the same note. Play both and tune until the beating is very slow.
  • The same works for the 5th and 4th strings, and the 4th and 3rd.

"Nearly" is deliberate. Harmonics give pure intervals, while the frets of a guitar are placed for equal temperament, which is very slightly different. Tuning by harmonics until the beats vanish completely leaves each string about two cents out, and the error adds up across the strings. Use harmonics to get close and to train your ear, then check with fretted notes or a tuner.

Tuning in fifths: violin, viola, cello

Bowed instruments are tuned by playing two neighbouring open strings together. A true fifth has a hollow, steady, ringing quality; one that is slightly narrow or wide beats slowly. Tune the A to a reference, then D against A, G against D, and so on.

How to practise

  1. Tune with the tuner. Then detune one string a little, eyes closed, and bring it back by ear. Check with the tuner.
  2. Use the tone generator against an open string and listen for beats as you turn the peg.
  3. Do this for two minutes at the start of each practice. Within a few weeks, you will hear a string that is out before you look at any needle.

Being a little out is normal

No instrument with frets or keys is perfectly in tune in every chord; equal temperament is a compromise that makes all keys equally usable. Good players tune carefully and then adjust by ear to the music they are playing. The ear is the final judge, which is the best reason to train it.
