Find your Sa: scales, sargam and tanpura tuning

In Indian music there is no fixed "do". The singer or player chooses a home note, Sa, that suits their voice or instrument, and every other note takes its place from it. A tuner made for Western music names notes as C, D, E, which is not much use when your teacher says "sing in kali ek". This page explains how the two systems meet, and lets the tuner show you swaras in the Sa you choose.

The seven swaras and their variants

An octave (saptak) has seven swaras: Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni. Sa and Pa never change. Re, Ga, Dha and Ni each have a lower form, called komal, and Ma has a higher form, called tivra. That makes twelve notes in all, which correspond to the twelve keys in one octave of a harmonium or keyboard.

What "kali 1" and "safed 1" mean

Harmonium players name the pitch of Sa by counting the white (safed) and black (kali) keys of the keyboard, starting from C:

So "safed ek" means Sa is on C, "kali ek" means Sa is on C sharp, "kali do" on D sharp, "safed paanch" on G, and so on. Singers also say "C sharp scale" or simply "one and a half". Frequencies are for the octave beginning at middle C; your own Sa may be an octave lower or higher.

Finding the Sa that suits your voice

  1. Choose "Sa on C" in the box above the tuner and tap Start.
  2. Hum or sing "aa" on the most comfortable low note you can hold steadily without strain. Note what the tuner shows.
  3. From that note, sing upwards as far as you comfortably can. Most untrained voices cover about an octave and a half.
  4. Your Sa should sit so that you can reach about half an octave below it, down to the lower Pa, and a full octave above it, to the upper Sa, without forcing.
  5. Try a Sa, sing Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni Sa up and down, and move the Sa a key lower or higher until both ends feel easy.

As a very rough guide only: many men settle somewhere between safed 1 and kali 2 (C to D sharp), and many women between kali 4 and kali 5 (G sharp to A sharp). Voices differ greatly, and a teacher who hears you is the best judge. Choose comfort over ambition; a Sa that is too high tires and can strain the voice.

These frequencies follow the equal steps of the harmonium and keyboard. Hindustani and Carnatic musicians often place some swaras a shade higher or lower than the keyboard does, by ear and according to the raga, which is one reason the tanpura, not the harmonium, is the reference for serious practice.

Tuning a tanpura

The tanpura has four strings (some have five or six) and plays only open strings, creating the drone against which all notes are heard. The usual tuning is:

StringNoteRemark
1stPa of the lower octaveTuned to Ma instead for ragas that have no Pa, and to Ni for some others
2nd and 3rd (jodi)Sa of the middle octaveThe pair must match exactly
4thSa of the lower octaveUsually a brass or bronze string
  1. Set your Sa in the box above and start the tuner.
  2. Tune the two middle strings to Sa. Pluck them together and remove any slow wavering between them.
  3. Tune the last string to the Sa one octave lower.
  4. Tune the first string to the lower Pa.
  5. Fine-tune with the beads below the bridge, then adjust the threads (jivari) under the strings on the bridge until each string has its full buzzing tone.

Pluck with the pad of the finger and let each string ring; the tanpura's rich overtones can make a tuner hesitate, so use the tuner to get close and your ear to finish. The lower Sa string may be too low for a phone microphone to hear well.

Electronic tanpura and reference tone

If you practise with an electronic tanpura or an app, set its pitch to the same key as your Sa. For a plain reference, the tone generator plays any note for as long as you like, and with your Sa chosen it names the notes as swaras.

Voice ranges given here are rough guides, not rules. If singing causes strain or hoarseness, stop, and consult your teacher.
