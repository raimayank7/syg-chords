Tuner

How a tuner works: hertz, notes and cents

A tuner does one thing: it measures how fast a sound is vibrating and tells you which note that is, and how far you are from it. The idea is simple, and knowing it makes you better at tuning, because you will understand what the needle is telling you and when not to trust it.

Pitch is speed of vibration

A string that is plucked swings back and forth at a steady rate, pushing the air into waves that reach the ear. The number of complete swings per second is the frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). A faster vibration sounds higher. The thick E string of a guitar vibrates about 82 times a second; the thin E string, two octaves higher, about 330 times.

Three things set the frequency of a string: its length, its weight and its tension. Tuning changes the third. Tighten the string and it vibrates faster; loosen it and it slows.

Notes, octaves and the number 2

Doubling a frequency raises a note by exactly one octave: 220 Hz, 440 Hz and 880 Hz are all the note A. Western music divides each octave into twelve equal steps called semitones, the distance from one fret to the next on a guitar. To go up one semitone, multiply the frequency by the twelfth root of 2, which is about 1.0595. Do that twelve times and you have doubled it. This system is called equal temperament, and it is what every ordinary tuner uses.

Notes are named with the letters A to G, with sharps in between, and a number for the octave. On this site middle C is C4 and the A above it is A4. The full list is on the note frequency chart.

Why A is 440

The whole system needs one fixed point. By international agreement that point is the A above middle C at 440 Hz, often called concert pitch or A440. Every other note follows from it by the rule above. Some orchestras tune a little higher, to 442 or 443, and players of early music often tune lower; that is why this tuner lets you change the reference. Unless you are playing with others who use a different pitch, leave it at 440.

Cents: the small print

A semitone is too coarse for tuning, so each one is divided into 100 cents. The needle of a tuner shows how many cents you are above (sharp) or below (flat) the nearest note.

  • Within about 5 cents, a single string sounds in tune to nearly everyone.
  • At 10 to 20 cents, chords begin to sound sour.
  • 50 cents is halfway to the next note.

Cents measure a ratio, not a number of hertz. One cent at the low E of a guitar is about a twentieth of a hertz; at the high E it is about a fifth of a hertz. This is why low strings need a steady hand and a patient ear.

How this page finds the pitch

When you tap Start, the browser passes the microphone's signal to the page as a stream of numbers. About eleven times a second the page takes a short slice, less than a tenth of a second long, and asks: by how many samples must I slide this wave along so that it lines up with itself again? That shift is the length of one vibration, and the frequency follows from it. The method works on the shape of the whole wave, which matters because a string does not produce a pure tone.

Overtones: why tuners sometimes jump

A vibrating string also vibrates in halves, thirds and quarters at the same time, producing fainter, higher notes called overtones or harmonics. They give each instrument its colour. Sometimes an overtone is louder than the main note, especially on thick strings heard through a small microphone, and a tuner may briefly show a note an octave or a fifth too high. Plucking more gently, nearer the middle of the string, and letting the note ring usually cures it.

Why a plucked string reads sharp at first

In the first instant after a hard pluck the string is stretched further than at rest, so its pitch is slightly high; it falls as the note fades. Tune to the steady part of the note, a second or so in, played at the strength you normally play.

What stays on your device

All of this arithmetic happens in your browser. The sound is examined and discarded; it is not recorded, saved or sent anywhere, and the microphone is released when you tap Stop or leave the page.
