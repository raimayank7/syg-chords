Why instruments go out of tune

You tuned yesterday, and today the instrument is out again. Nothing is broken. A stringed instrument is a balance between the pull of the strings and the resistance of wood, and anything that disturbs either side changes the pitch. Once you know the causes, most of them are easy to manage.

1. New strings stretch

By far the commonest cause. A new string keeps lengthening under tension for hours or days, and as it lengthens it goes flat. Nylon and other soft strings, as on classical guitars and ukuleles, stretch most and can take a week to settle; steel strings settle within a day or two.

What to do: after fitting a string, tune it, then hook a finger under it near the middle and pull it gently away from the fretboard a few centimetres, working along its length. Retune and repeat three or four times. Then expect to retune often for a few days.

2. The string is slipping at the post

If a string is not secured well at the tuning post, it creeps under tension.

What to do: when stringing, pass the string through the hole, leave a little slack, and wind so that the turns go neatly downwards on the post, two or three turns for wound strings and a few more for thin plain ones. The turns should trap the string end. Too many turns are as bad as too few, since a thick coil keeps settling.

3. Temperature and humidity

Wood swells when the air is damp and shrinks when it is dry; strings lengthen when warm and shorten when cold. In the Indian climate, with a humid monsoon and a dry winter or an air-conditioned room, instruments move all year. Carrying a guitar from a cold car into a warm hall will change its tuning within minutes.

What to do: keep the instrument in its case when it is not being played, away from windows, heaters, coolers and direct sun. Never leave it in a parked vehicle. After moving between very different places, let it rest in its case for a while, then tune.

4. Strings catching in the nut

The nut is the slotted piece at the top of the fretboard. If a slot grips the string, the string tightens unevenly: you tune, the string sticks, and later it slips with a "ping" and goes sharp or flat.

What to do: when changing strings, rub the point of a soft pencil in each slot; graphite is a dry lubricant. If the problem persists, a repair shop can ease the slots.

5. Old strings

Strings wear, corrode from the moisture of the fingers and collect dirt. An old string vibrates unevenly: it may be in tune when open and out of tune when fretted, or sound dull however you tune it.

What to do: wipe the strings with a dry cloth after playing, and wash your hands before. Change strings when they look discoloured, feel rough or will not hold a note. How often depends entirely on how much you play.

6. Pegs and machine heads

  • Geared tuners on guitars seldom fail, but a loose button screw lets the peg turn too freely. Tighten it slightly.
  • Friction pegs on ukuleles and violins hold only by grip. Push a violin peg gently inwards as you turn; tighten the end screw of a ukulele friction peg a little if it slips.

7. How you tune

A string lowered to a note often drops further afterwards, because slack remains in the gear and between the nut and the post. Always tune up to the note. If you overshoot, go below and come up again.

8. How you play

Hard strumming, string bending and heavy use of a vibrato arm all pull strings about. Pressing too hard on the frets, or using a capo that clamps too tightly, makes notes sharp even when the open strings are perfect. Retune after putting on a capo.

9. In tune open, out of tune up the neck

If the open strings are right but chords higher up sound wrong, the instrument's intonation is off: the bridge is in the wrong place, the strings sit too high, or the strings are worn. Check by comparing each string's 12th-fret note with its 12th-fret harmonic on the tuner; they should match. On instruments with a movable bridge, such as banjos and mandolins, the bridge may simply have shifted. Otherwise, new strings are the first thing to try, and a repairer the second.

Habits that keep you in tune

  • Tune every time you pick the instrument up; it takes one minute with the tuner.
  • Tune up to each note.
  • Store the instrument in its case.
  • Wipe the strings after playing.
  • Change one string at a time, so that the neck stays under tension.
