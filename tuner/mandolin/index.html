Mandolin tuning

A mandolin has eight strings in four pairs, called courses. Both strings of a pair are tuned to the same note, and the four pairs are tuned G, D, A, E from lowest to highest, exactly the notes of a violin. This is why violin music can be played on a mandolin, and why the mandolin has found a home in Indian film and classical music alongside the violin.

The difficulty: two strings, one note

If you pick a pair together and the two strings are slightly different, the tuner hears a blur and the needle wanders. So tune one string of each pair at a time:

  1. Tap Start tuner.
  2. Rest a fingertip on one string of the G pair to stop it sounding.
  3. Pick the other string and tune it until the display says "In tune".
  4. Now stop that string and tune its partner in the same way.
  5. Pick the pair together and listen. If you hear a slow wavering, one string is still slightly off; adjust it until the two sound like one.
  6. Repeat for the D, A and E pairs.

Listen for the shimmer

Two strings very nearly but not exactly at the same pitch produce a regular pulsing, called beats. The closer they are, the slower the pulse; when they agree, it stops and the note sounds single and clear. Tuning the second string of each pair to the first by ear, until the beating disappears, is quicker than using the tuner twice, and it trains your ear. See tuning by ear.

Which peg is which?

With eight pegs it is easy to turn the wrong one. Follow each string from the bridge to its post with your finger before turning. On most mandolins the pegs on the side nearest your face serve the G and D pairs, and those on the far side the A and E pairs, but check your own instrument.

Tension and the bridge

Steel mandolin strings are short and tight, so a small turn of the peg makes a large change in pitch. Turn slowly. The bridge of most mandolins is not glued; it is held in place by the strings. When changing strings, replace them one at a time so that the bridge does not move, since its position decides whether the instrument plays in tune up the neck.

Indian classical mandolin

In Carnatic music the mandolin is often played with single strings instead of pairs and tuned to the performer's tonic, for example to Sa and Pa in alternation. For such tunings choose your Sa on the find your Sa page and use the chromatic tuner, which will then show swara names.
