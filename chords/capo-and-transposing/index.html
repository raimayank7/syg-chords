What transposing is

To transpose is to move a piece of music to a higher or lower key without changing anything else. Every chord moves by the same number of semitones, so the chords keep their relationships and the song sounds the same, only higher or lower. People transpose for two reasons: to bring a song into a comfortable range for the voice, and to swap awkward chords for easier ones.

How to transpose by hand

  1. Write the twelve notes in a circle or a line: C, C♯, D, D♯, E, F, F♯, G, G♯, A, A♯, B.
  2. Decide the distance, for example two semitones up.
  3. Move the root letter of every chord by that distance. Leave the rest of the chord name alone: A minor seventh, moved up two, is B minor seventh.
  4. G, E minor, C, D becomes A, F♯ minor, D, E.

The key chart gives another way: find the chords' Roman numerals in the old key and read the same numerals in the new one.

The capo

A capo is a clamp that presses all the strings of a guitar or ukulele at one fret, acting as a movable nut. Every fret raises the pitch by one semitone. You go on playing the chord shapes you know, and the capo makes them sound higher. It is the easy way to raise the key of a song for a singer, or to play in a key such as B flat or E flat with open-chord shapes instead of barre chords.

The table is for major shapes; minor and seventh shapes move in exactly the same way, so an A minor shape with the capo on the 2nd fret sounds B minor. Read it backwards to choose a capo position: to play in B♭ with easy shapes, use G shapes with the capo at the 3rd fret, or A shapes with the capo at the 1st.

Choosing a key for your voice

  1. Find the highest note of the song. If it strains you, the key is too high; if the lowest note disappears into a growl, it is too low.
  2. Move the key down or up two semitones at a time and try again.
  3. Men and women often need keys about four or five semitones apart for the same song, so a songbook key that suits one may not suit the other.
  4. When you find the key, write the new chords on your sheet.

Using a capo well

  • Place it just behind the fret, not on top of it and not in the middle of the space.
  • Use only as much pressure as stops the buzzing; too much pulls the strings sharp.
  • Retune after putting it on, and again after taking it off. Our neighbour site Tuner helps.
  • With two guitars, one can play open shapes without a capo and the other the same chords with a capo higher up, for a fuller sound.

Sharps or flats?

Each black-key note has two names. Keys with flats in their name or signature (F, B♭, E♭, A♭) are written with flats; most others with sharps. Tick "Use flats" in the tool if the result looks more natural that way. The sound is the same.
