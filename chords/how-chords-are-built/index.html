Chords

How chords are built

A chord is three or more notes sounded together. There are hundreds of chord names, but they are all made from a few simple recipes. Learn the recipes and you can build any chord on any instrument, work out the notes of a chord you have never seen, and understand why chords with different names feel the way they do.

Step 1: semitones

The smallest step in ordinary music is the semitone: from one fret to the next on a guitar, or from one key to the very next key, black or white, on a keyboard. Two semitones make a whole tone. There are twelve different notes before the names repeat:

C, C♯, D, D♯, E, F, F♯, G, G♯, A, A♯, B, and C again.

The sharps can also be written as flats: C♯ is the same note as D♭, and so on. Notice that there is no black key between E and F, or between B and C; those pairs are a semitone apart.

Step 2: the major scale

Chord recipes are written with numbers that refer to the notes of the major scale starting on the chord's root. A major scale climbs by this pattern of steps: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. Starting on C it uses only the white keys:

In a recipe, a flat sign before a number lowers that note by a semitone, and a sharp sign raises it. So "b3" means the third note of the major scale, lowered by one semitone.

Why major and minor feel different

The only difference between a major and a minor chord is the middle note. In a major chord the third is four semitones above the root; in a minor chord it is three. That one semitone is the difference between bright and dark. Suspended chords remove the third altogether, which is why they sound neither happy nor sad, and power chords do the same.

Stacking thirds

Another way to see it: chords are built by taking every other note of a scale. From C, skip D and take E, skip F and take G: C, E, G. Keep going, skip A and take B, and you have the four-note seventh chord C, E, G, B. Each step in the stack is a third, either major (four semitones) or minor (three):

  • major third + minor third = major chord
  • minor third + major third = minor chord
  • minor third + minor third = diminished chord
  • major third + major third = augmented chord

Chords that belong to a key

Build a three-note chord on each note of a major scale, using only the notes of that scale, and you get seven chords that sound right together. In every major key the pattern is the same: the chords on notes 1, 4 and 5 are major, those on 2, 3 and 6 are minor, and the one on 7 is diminished. Musicians write them with Roman numerals: I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii°. The full table for all twelve keys is on chords in every key.

The same notes, different names

  • C6 (C, E, G, A) has the same notes as A minor seventh (A, C, E, G). The bass note decides the name.
  • Csus2 (C, D, G) has the same notes as Gsus4 (G, C, D).
  • C augmented, E augmented and A♭ augmented share their three notes.

So a chord name is partly a description of which note is treated as home.

Leaving notes out and doubling them

A guitar has six strings and a major chord only three notes, so guitar chords repeat some notes in different octaves. Equally, in four-note chords players often leave out the fifth, because it adds the least to the chord's character; the root, third and seventh identify it. The order of the notes from bottom to top can also change without changing the chord's name; that is called an inversion.
