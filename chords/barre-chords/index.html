Barre chords: two shapes for every chord

A barre chord uses the first finger laid flat across the strings to do the job of the nut, so that an open-chord shape can be moved anywhere on the neck. Learn two shapes and you can play every major and minor chord there is, including the awkward ones such as F, B flat, B minor and F sharp minor that have no easy open form. Every guitarist finds them hard at first. Nearly all of the difficulty is technique, not strength.

The idea

Play an open E major chord. Now imagine sliding the whole chord, nut and all, one fret up the neck. Your first finger becomes the nut, lying across all six strings at the 1st fret, and the other three fingers make the E shape just above it. The result is F major. Slide it up two more frets and it is G major. The shape never changes; only its position does.

Shape 1: the E shape (root on the 6th string)

The name of the chord is the note under your first finger on the 6th string. For the minor chord, lift the second finger; for the seventh chord, lift the little finger.

Shape 2: the A shape (root on the 5th string)

This is the open A major chord moved up. The first finger covers strings 5 to 1, and the chord is named by the note on the 5th string. The 6th string is not played. Many players fret the three notes above the barre with the third finger flattened across them.

Finding any chord

You need to know the notes along the two thickest strings. This table gives the fret for each root:

So B flat major can be played as the E shape at the 6th fret or the A shape at the 1st fret. Choose whichever is nearer to the chords around it in the song.

How to make the barre ring

  • Use the bony edge of the finger. Roll the first finger slightly towards the nut so that its harder outer edge, not the soft pad, presses the strings.
  • Get close to the fret. Lay the finger right behind the fret wire, parallel to it. Half a centimetre back needs twice the pressure.
  • Thumb behind the neck, roughly opposite the first or second finger, pressing like a clamp. Do not hook it over the top.
  • Pull with the arm. Let the weight of your arm pull the fingers gently into the neck, with the elbow near your side. The strength should not come from squeezing with the thumb alone.
  • Mind the crease. If one string is dead, it may be sitting in the crease of a finger joint. Shift the finger up or down a few millimetres.
  • Only some strings need the barre. In the E shape, the barre finger actually sounds only the 6th, 2nd and 1st strings; the other fingers cover the rest. Concentrate the pressure there.

A way to practise

  1. Start at the 5th or 7th fret, not the 1st. The frets are closer together and the strings easier to press there.
  2. Lay the barre alone and pick each string. Adjust until all six ring.
  3. Add the other fingers and pick each string again.
  4. Hold for five seconds, release, shake the hand out and repeat five times.
  5. Work down one fret every few days until you reach F at the 1st fret, the hardest position.

Two or three minutes a day is enough. Stop if the hand aches; the muscles between thumb and fingers need a few weeks to build.

If it still buzzes

  • Check the tuning and the strings. Old or heavy strings and a high "action" (strings far above the fretboard) make barres much harder. A guitar repairer can lower the action on most instruments.
  • Try a capo at the 3rd fret and practise the shapes above it. If chords ring easily there, the guitar's nut may be too high.
  • Classical guitars with wide necks, and cheap guitars with high strings, are the hardest to barre. It may not be you.

Easier stand-ins while you learn

  • Small F: play only the four thinnest strings, with the first finger barring just the 1st and 2nd strings at the 1st fret. It is shown on the F major page.
  • Power chords: the two or three thickest strings of the barre shape, without the barre. See any power chord.
  • A capo lets you play open shapes in other keys; see capo and transposing.

Why it is worth it

Barre chords free you from the nut. Every chord becomes available, songs in "difficult" keys become ordinary, and because the shapes are movable you start to see the neck as a map. They also let you stop the strings ringing by relaxing the hand, which is the secret of tight rhythm playing.
