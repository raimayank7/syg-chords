Chords in every key

A key is a family of seven notes, and the chords made from those notes sound right together. If you know which chords belong to a key, you can accompany a tune by ear, guess the next chord of a song, and move a song to a key that suits your voice. The pattern is the same in every major key: the chords built on the first, fourth and fifth notes are major, those on the second, third and sixth are minor, and the one on the seventh is diminished. Musicians number them with Roman numerals, capitals for major and small letters for minor.

Minor keys

Each minor key uses the same seven chords as the major key three semitones above it, its relative major, but treats the minor chord as home. In practice the v chord of a minor key is very often changed to a major or seventh chord (V or V7), because that gives a stronger pull back to the home chord: in A minor, E or E7 instead of E minor.

Common progressions in every major key

A progression is a sequence of chords. These three patterns account for a very large share of popular, folk and devotional music. The first uses only the three main chords. The second adds the vi chord. The third is the basic cadence of jazz, shown here with seventh chords.

How to use the chart

  • To find a song's key, look at its chords, especially the first and last. The key whose row contains all of them is very likely the key.
  • To work out a song by ear, find the key, then try I, IV and V first; one of them is right most of the time. Then try vi and ii.
  • To change key, replace each chord by the chord with the same Roman numeral in the new row. A song that goes G, C, D in the key of G goes A, D, E in the key of A. The transposing tool does this for you.
  • To write your own tune, pick a row and stay inside it. Start and end on I.

Chords from outside the key

Real music often borrows. The most common visitors are the V chord turned into a seventh, the major chord a whole step below the home chord (B♭ in the key of C), and the major version of a chord that the key makes minor (E major in the key of C, leading to A minor). When a song contains one chord that is not in its row, it has not changed key; it has borrowed a colour.

Easy keys on each instrument

  • Guitar: G, D, A, E and C major, and E minor and A minor, need mostly open chords. See first guitar chords.
  • Ukulele: C, F and G major and A minor. See first ukulele chords.
  • Keyboard: C major and A minor use only white keys; G and F major need one black key each.

How the chords of a key arise from its scale is explained in how chords are built.
